Inter-subject correlation, explained

When a film grips an audience, viewers' brains start to move together. Inter-subject correlation (ISC) measures how closely they do.

What ISC measures

Record the same signal (an fMRI voxel, an EEG component, pupil size or skin conductance) from several people watching the same film. ISC is the correlation between their time courses. A value near 1 means everyone's signal rises and falls together; a value near 0 means their responses are unrelated.

How it is calculated

There are two common versions, both shown in the simulator above:

  • Pairwise ISC: correlate every pair of viewers and average the results.
  • Leave-one-out ISC: correlate each viewer with the average of everyone else, then average across viewers. Because the group average cancels out private noise, this value is usually higher.

For EEG, researchers often first find the components that are most correlated across viewers, using a method called correlated component analysis, and compute ISC on those.

What high and low ISC mean for film

High ISC tends to appear during suspense, clear action and moments where editing directs attention. It drops during ambiguous scenes, when attention wanders, or when viewers bring very different interpretations. Studies have linked higher EEG ISC to stronger engagement and better memory for what was shown.

A caveat: ISC shows that responses are shared, not why. Two people can be closely synchronised for different reasons, so ISC is usually combined with ratings, eye tracking or content analysis.

Try it with real data

Several open datasets record brain or body signals while people watch films, which makes them ideal for computing ISC yourself.
