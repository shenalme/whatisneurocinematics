Neurocinematics glossary

Short, plain-English definitions of the terms you will meet in film, brain and emotion research.

ABCEHILNSTVf
Affective computing
The study and design of systems that recognise, interpret or respond to human emotion, for example from facial expressions, voice or physiological signals.
Arousal
The intensity of an emotional state, from calm to excited, independent of whether the feeling is pleasant or unpleasant.
Attentional synchrony
The tendency of many viewers to look at the same place on screen at the same time, often produced by composition, motion and editing.
Average shot length (ASL)
A film's running time divided by its number of shots; the standard measure of editing pace. See the shot length calculator.
BOLD signal
Blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal: the change in blood oxygenation measured by fMRI, used as an indirect marker of neural activity.
Continuity editing
The dominant Hollywood editing style, designed to make cuts feel invisible by keeping space, time and action consistent across shots.
Correlated component analysis
A technique that finds combinations of EEG channels whose signals are most correlated across viewers, often used before computing ISC.
Cutting rate
The number of cuts per minute; the inverse view of average shot length.
EEG
Electroencephalography: recording the brain's electrical activity through electrodes on the scalp, with millisecond timing.
Electrodermal activity (EDA)
Changes in the skin's electrical conductance caused by sweat gland activity; a common marker of physiological arousal. Also called galvanic skin response.
Event-related potential (ERP)
An EEG response time-locked to a specific event, such as a cut or a sudden sound, found by averaging many repetitions.
Eye tracking
Measuring where and how long a viewer looks, using cameras that follow the pupils.
fMRI
Functional magnetic resonance imaging: a brain scanning method that tracks blood flow changes with high spatial detail but slow timing.
Heart rate variability (HRV)
Variation in the time between heartbeats, linked to stress, arousal and regulation of the nervous system.
Inter-subject correlation (ISC)
The correlation between different viewers' brain or body signals over time; a measure of how much a stimulus drives a shared response. See the ISC explainer.
Leave-one-out ISC
A version of ISC that correlates each viewer with the average of all other viewers.
Naturalistic paradigm
A research design that uses rich, real-world stimuli such as films or stories instead of simple, repeated lab tasks.
Neurocinematics
The study of how films shape viewers' brain activity, attention and emotion. See the beginner's guide.
Saliency
How strongly a part of an image stands out and attracts the eye, due to contrast, motion, colour or faces.
Spectatorship
In film studies, the experience and position of the viewer: how audiences perceive, interpret and respond to films.
Temporal receptive window
The length of time over which a brain region integrates information; sensory areas respond to moments, while higher areas track whole scenes and stories.
Valence
Whether an emotional state is pleasant or unpleasant, independent of its intensity.
